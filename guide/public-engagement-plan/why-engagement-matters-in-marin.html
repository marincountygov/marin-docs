Why engagement matters in Marin

Marin County isn't one community — effective engagement starts with knowing which community you're actually in.

Updated August 28, 2026 at 1:59 PM PDT

Download PDF

Not one community

West Marin contains communities with different histories, priorities, and relationships with County government. The Canal neighborhood and downtown San Rafael are a few miles apart and worlds different in terms of what residents need from an engagement process. Marin City carries a history with local government that shapes how residents show up, and whether they show up at all.

Do the homework before outreach begins. Talk to staff who have relationships in the area. Connect with CBOs and community leaders who already have trust there. Understand what promises have been made, whether they were kept, and what residents may need to see before they believe this process will be different.

Project relevance

Engagement only works if the project, program, or initiative genuinely matters to the communities being asked to participate. When a community's lived experiences, local knowledge, and history with government aren't woven into the engagement strategy, the process can feel predetermined. People sense when they've been invited to validate a decision rather than shape it.

The consequences are real: participation drops, and the same familiar faces keep showing up while others disengage entirely. Communities that felt unheard don't forget it, and that frustration surfaces later as organized opposition or pressure on elected officials. Closing the loop every time is one of the most direct ways to counter this. See Step 6 for guidance on the report-back process.

Best practice
  • Community members' knowledge and lived experience have real value. Where budget allows, stipends signal that the county understands what it's asking of people.
  • Sometimes the most effective engagement isn't led by the county at all. Knowing when to step back and support a trusted partner rather than lead is a skill.

The Board of Supervisors and district offices

Before external engagement begins on any project touching Board priorities or district constituents, brief the relevant supervisor's office. Supervisors and their aides hear things that don't make it through formal channels. Briefing the district office isn't about getting political direction on technical work — it's about making sure engagement efforts are grounded in what's happening in the community.

County staff

Current staff are an often underrecognized engagement asset. Institutional knowledge, existing community relationships, and cultural understanding bring something no outreach strategy can fully replicate. Some departments don't just serve the community — they are the community: MCFL, HHS, and Parks operate through direct, ongoing relationships with residents across every neighborhood and life circumstance.

Trusted partners

Trusted partners are individuals, organizations, and institutions with established relationships, credibility, and trust within a community. People are far more likely to show up when the invitation comes from someone they already know. Common trusted partners in Marin include CBOs, faith-based organizations, schools and parent groups, TAY programs, cultural and affinity organizations, nonprofits, advocacy groups, tenant associations, neighborhood leaders, senior centers, local businesses, health clinics, Community Resilience Teams, promotores, tribal representatives, and community ambassadors.

The Clerk of the Board is a critical and often overlooked partner for any engagement effort involving a standing advisory body — if a project involves forming any advisory board, commission, or working group, the Clerk should be one of the first calls. The Municipal Services Managers for West Marin and Marin City are irreplaceable for any project touching either community — consult them before external outreach begins, always and every time.

Note

What's captured here reflects what the County already knows so far. Phase Two will bring together an internal working group to examine current practices and surface gaps. Phase Three takes this work into the community, neighborhood by neighborhood. This section will grow as that work unfolds.
